on Real-World Distributed Massive MIMO CSI Phillip Stephan , Florian Euchner , Stephan ten Brink Institute of Telecommunications, Pfaffenwaldring 47, University of Stuttgart, 70569 Stuttgart, Germany {stephan,euchner,tenbrink}@inue.uni-stuttgart.de Abstract—Distributed massive MIMO is considered a key advancement for improving the performance of next-generation wireless telecommunication systems. However, its efficacy in scenarios involving user mobility is limited due to channel aging. To address this challenge, channel prediction techniques are investigated to forecast future channel state information (CSI) based on previous estimates. We propose a new channel prediction method based on channel charting, a self-supervised learning technique that reconstructs a physically meaningful latent representation of the radio environment using similarity relationships between CSI samples. Our method is validated on a real-world distributed massive MIMO dataset, and compared to a Wiener predictor and the outdated CSI in terms of achievable sum rate. C ONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK We demonstrated on real-world distributed massive MIMO measurements that channel prediction can be performed based on a channel chart. The CSI predicted with our method is more resilient over time than the Wiener predictor and the outdated CSI, although the Wiener predictor performs better for short prediction horizons. However, channel charting is still a relatively new research area with a lot of potential for algorithmic improvements, which will likely enhance the prediction accuracy. Further, latent space prediction holds potential for optimization, since linear extrapolation of channel chart positions is an idealized assumption that is less effective for curved trajectories. A. Dissimilarity Metric-Based Channel Charting Our approach for dissimilarity metric-based channel chart- ing, as described in [20], relies on dissimilarities di,j (”pseudo- distances”) between any pair of datapoints with indices i and j in the training set, to learn a channel chart jointly for all arrays. In particular, the geodesic, fused dissimilarity metric [20] is employed, which computes dissimilarities based on the angle-delay profile (ADP) at all BS antenna arrays and information about timestamp differences. Learning the FCF, which maps the high-dimensional CSI to the low-dimensional latent space, i.e., the channel chart, is an optimization problem that minimizes the error between the dissimilarity matrix and the point-to-point distances in the channel chart. Since no ground truth information is available, the learned channel chart embodies the radio environment in a transformed version of the physical coordinates. We implement the FCF as a deep neural network (DNN) CΘ : f(l) → z(l), where f(l) is a CSI feature derived from H(l) serving as DNN input, and z(l) is the respective estimated channel chart position. As described in [20], we use sample autocorrelations of time-domain CSI as input features. The advantage of using a DNN to model the FCF is its ability to infer channel chart positions for new datapoints without retraining. During training, the DNN CΘ is embedded in a Siamese network, and the related Siamese loss function is applied as LSiamese = XL−1 i=1 XL j=i+1 di,j − ∥z(i) − z(j)∥2 2 di,j + β , (3) with β being a hyperparameter to weight either the absolute squared error or the normalized squared error higher. Evaluation of the Channel Chart The quality of a channel chart is typically evaluated using performance metrics from dimensionality reduction. The met- rics Continuity (CT) and Trustworthiness (TW) [21] measure the preservation of local neighborhood dependencies in the channel chart, ranging from 0 to 1, whith higher values indi- cating better performance. Kruskal’s stress (KS) [22] evaluates the global structure, also ranging from 0 to 1, but lower values being preferred. Additionally, the mean absolute error (MAE) can be computed after applying an optimal affine transformation to the channel chart positions [23].
